# 3D craft

Use for spatial characters, props, articulated objects, and rigid or deformable
motion. Tool controls and a geometrically correct model do not ensure a convincing
performance. Start with [principles](principles.md), and use [styles](styles.md)
when realism is not the target.

## Posing and balance

Build a readable line of action and a specific intention. Check silhouette,
foreshortening, tangencies, facial visibility, and the space around a gesture
through the delivery camera. Asymmetry can clarify weight distribution, but do
not break intentional symmetry or a symmetrical mechanism.

For a static unsupported biped stance, the center-of-mass projection generally
needs to lie within its support area. During dynamic action it can leave that
area: momentum, external supports, and the next contact explain how the body
recovers. Do not "fix" a run by balancing every sampled pose as if it were still.

When lifting or carrying a prop, consider the combined body and load. Show the
preparation of support, force through hands and feet, and a change of posture
under load. Mass reads through effort and acceleration, not just a slow curve.

## Contacts and constraints

Establish a contact schedule: which surface supports which part, when it plants,
when it rolls, and when it releases. Fix contacts before fine overlap.

- A foot planted on a static floor should not drift in world space. On a moving
  platform, preserve the intended contact relative to that platform.
- Heel-to-toe motion changes the support pivot; a foot is not glued flat for
  every walking phase. Account for purposeful slides and skids.
- A hand gripping a prop must maintain the designed grip relationship until
  release. Review hand, wrist, prop, and body together.
- Preserve the visible world pose across parenting, constraint, or IK/FK
  changes. Use the chosen tool's guidance for implementation; a one-frame pop
  is usually a continuity failure rather than an acting choice.
- Check the relevant surface, not just a rig handle or bounding-box origin.
  Contact can fail even when the control appears stationary.

For loops, coordinate root speed and stride length. A treadmill walk and a
traveling walk require different world-position checks. Do not correct one by
quietly turning it into the other.

## Paths, pivots, and rotations

Track geometry landmarks: nose, fingertips, heel, prop edge. A clean local
translation channel may produce a lumpy world arc through the hierarchy.
Adjust breakdowns, spaces, and pivots based on the resulting path.

Set the pivot to the physical or designed action: a door hinges, a wheel rotates
about its axle, a rolling body changes its contact relationship. With rolling
without slip, distance and angle are related by `distance = radius * angle`
(angle in radians); stylized slip should be intentional.

Specify the intended rotation route. A shortest-path rotation is useful for a
small reorientation but wrong for a deliberate full spin. Watch wraparound,
Euler discontinuities, and unintended axis flips. Quaternions can help represent
orientation without some Euler problems but do not automatically preserve
multiple revolutions or choose the desired choreography.

## Overlap and deformation

Animate the driver and major body action first, then decide how connected parts
react. Consider the whole chain: pelvis, torso, shoulders, arms, hands, and any
attachments. Avoid mechanically offsetting every channel by the same number
of frames; lead and lag follow anatomy and intent.

Allow appropriate recovery after contact, while respecting planted constraints.
A landing's feet may stop while the body's compression continues. Do not make
hair, cloth, and skin share the same oscillation merely because one curve looks
pleasant. Damping, stiffness, and range should fit the material and shot.

Review silhouettes and internal volume during extreme poses. Watch unintended
joint collapse, intersections, scale inheritance, and texture stretching. Favor
posing and articulation for rigid objects. Deliberate cheats for one camera are
valid when the delivery and intended reuse permit them; multi-view or interactive
assets need review from their actual viewing range.

## Simulation and polish

Use simulation where useful, but set the main action and dramatic timing first.
Check initial state, settling, and reproducibility when evaluating arbitrary
frames. A technically plausible result can still obscure a pose or dominate
attention. Art-direct amplitude or timing within the chosen workflow.

Inspect low-cost playback from the delivery camera before expensive rendering.
Then isolate geometry landmarks and contacts. Remove only unintended noise;
do not smooth away a weight change, impact, or a deliberately stepped pose.
Review the final composite too, since blur, framing, lighting, and camera motion
can hide or exaggerate motion defects. See [review](review.md).
