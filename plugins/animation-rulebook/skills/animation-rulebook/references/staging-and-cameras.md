# Staging, acting beats, and cameras

## Give the shot a readable intention

Identify the information the viewer should notice first and what changes by the
end. That may be a gesture, physical event, emotional discovery, product feature,
or deliberate feeling of disorder. Establish a focal hierarchy rather than
making every element compete for attention.

Use framing, silhouette, contrast, depth, gaze, and relative motion together.
A secondary object's fast movement can attract more attention than the largest
subject. Check at the intended display size, where a subtle face or thin hand
may disappear. Keep the important contact or expression visible unless concealing
it is the point of the shot.

Give information time to register. Separate important beats enough that the
audience can connect cause and effect. Simultaneous action is valid when its
relationship is readable; there is no requirement to finish every movement
before another begins.

## Connect thought to action

Place changes of attention and intention deliberately. An eye shift, head turn,
breath, posture change, and hand action can have distinct timing, but there is
no mandatory eyes-head-body formula for every response. A reflex, distraction,
or concealment may use another order.

Let a supporting gesture clarify the primary action. If a character must reveal
an object from a pocket, establish the relevant hand and pocket clearly enough
that the reveal makes sense. If wiping a tear matters, avoid hiding the whole
emotional expression at its most important moment.

Hold a pose when it conveys thought; avoid filling every pause with idle motion.
For dialogue or music, relate animation to phrase meaning and selected accents,
not mechanically to every syllable or beat.

## Motivate camera movement

Choose the camera's job: observe, follow, reveal, emphasize, establish scale, or
change the viewer's relationship to the subject. A locked camera is often enough.
Do not add constant orbiting, zooming, shake, or drifting merely to make a still
composition appear animated.

Coordinate subject and camera so the intended relative motion remains visible.
A perfectly tracking camera can hide the speed of a run; backgrounds, framing,
and parallax may need to carry that information. Conversely, moving both camera
and subject aggressively can make an otherwise clear gesture unreadable.

For an orbit, dolly, pan, or push, decide the subject anchor, path, speed profile,
and start/end framing. Look-at controls can produce accidental roll, flips,
or a rigidly locked feeling. Check horizon and orientation continuity along the
full path. Do not assume a smooth camera curve guarantees a comfortable image.

Use shake or impact motion as an accent with a cause, scale, and recovery. Preserve
readability, especially in close views and interfaces. Avoid random high-frequency
chatter as a substitute for impact staging.

## Cuts and continuity

Maintain screen direction, geography, and attention across cuts when continuity
is intended. Direction changes can be purposeful, but reorient the audience when
needed. Match the part of the action that matters, not merely the numeric pose
of a rig. A cut can intentionally skip time or reverse expectations.

Check whether a new shot begins with enough information to understand the next
beat. A close-up may communicate expression while a wider view is needed to read
the physical cause. Centering and thirds are composition tools, not compulsory
positions for the subject.

## Eye trace, rhythm, and sound across a sequence

Before a cut, note where the audience is likely attending; after it, decide
where the next useful information appears. A matching gaze target, action,
or entry direction can connect discontinuous views. Tim J. Smith's
[attentional account of continuity](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21187/)
supports treating attention as part of editing, beyond literal geometric matching.
This is a theory of viewing, not a fixed rule that every focal point must occupy
the same pixels across a cut. A deliberate disorientation can serve the scene.

Evaluate phrase lengths across shots: a build, accent, reaction, and resolution
need not share the same duration or speed. A slow scene is not one in which
every part moves slowly; it can contain a brief decisive change inside stillness.
Similarly, constant maximum activity leaves little room for emphasis. Compare
rhythmic alternatives in a rough sequence before refining individual shots.

For sound, distinguish causal sync from emotional accompaniment. A visible
footfall or close impact often needs a deliberate contact relationship; music
can organize larger phrases without making every movement hit every beat.
Let dialogue and reaction timing coexist with the sound edit. Check the actual
combined playback, since a frame-accurate animation alone cannot establish that
the audio track is aligned correctly.

## Verification

Watch once at delivery speed to identify what attracts attention. Then inspect
silhouettes, occlusion, important contacts, and relative motion. For text, test
the actual copy and audience rather than using a universal read-time multiplier.
If a camera change creates a motion defect, assess camera and subject together
before retiming the performance. See [review](review.md).
