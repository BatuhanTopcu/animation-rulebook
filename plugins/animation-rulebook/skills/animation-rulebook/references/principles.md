# The 12 principles as working decisions

Use these together, but do not force all twelve into every shot. Start with
the action's meaning, physical character, and chosen visual language. The
foundation is the user-supplied account of Thomas and Johnston's principles,
explained in Alan Becker's series; see [sources](sources.md).

This is the classical foundation section of a broader craft library. It does
not replace guidance on performance, observation, locomotion, editing, effects,
procedural continuity, or perception. Use the [skill's routing table](../SKILL.md)
to reach those domains rather than trying to explain every defect through these
twelve labels.

## 1. Squash and stretch

**Decide:** how flexible the subject is and where energy compresses or extends
its shape. A soft body may compress on contact; a rigid articulated figure can
fold and extend its pose without deforming its parts.

**Recognize and repair:** unintended size inflation makes deformation feel
like growth. Balance stretch across dimensions and preserve the contact point.
For a flat shape scaled in two axes, `sx * sy = 1` preserves area. For a 3D
volume, `sx * sy * sz = 1`; stretching one axis by `k` and scaling both others
by `1/sqrt(k)` is a simple volume-preserving starting point. These are geometric
constraints, not a complete deformation model. A projected 3D silhouette need
not preserve its 2D area.

**Exception:** rigid material, a restrained style, or an intentional size change
may call for little or no squash. Deformation should support velocity and
contact, not run constantly through the entire trajectory.

## 2. Anticipation

**Decide:** what preparation supplies force or directs attention. Weight shifts,
a glance, a breath, and a hand positioning itself can all prepare a beat.

**Recognize and repair:** an action feels unmotivated, weak, or easy to miss.
Clarify the preparation and its relationship to the main action; do not simply
insert a backward movement before everything.

**Exception:** surprise, reflex, external impact, and immediate interaction
feedback may intentionally lack visible preparation. A reaction to an unseen
cause should not announce that cause prematurely.

## 3. Staging

**Decide:** what the viewer needs to understand now. Pose, framing, contrast,
occlusion, and relative motion should make that idea readable.

**Recognize and repair:** competing actions divide attention or a silhouette
hides the gesture. Reduce competing motion, clarify negative space, reframe,
or separate beats. Test small and at playback speed.

**Exception:** purposeful chaos and simultaneous action are valid if the
intended experience remains clear. Centering, thirds, and one-at-a-time action
are options, not laws. See [staging](staging-and-cameras.md).

## 4. Straight ahead and pose to pose

**Decide:** which aspects need planned endpoints and which evolve through time.
Block a jump with keys and contacts; develop a trailing cloth shape or flame
through successive states inside those timing constraints.

**Recognize and repair:** improvisation drifts away from the planned endpoint,
or interpolation produces a bland route. Establish destination poses and
breakdowns, then evolve the connecting motion deliberately.

**Exception:** these methods can be mixed. A simulation still needs direction,
initial conditions, and review; pose-to-pose does not mean trusting automatic
in-betweens. Key, extreme, and breakdown terminology can vary by workflow.

## 5. Follow-through and overlapping action

**Decide:** what leads, what lags, and how transferred energy settles. Consider
attachments, stiffness, inertia, damping, and external forces.

**Recognize and repair:** everything stops together or every part copies the
same curve. Introduce meaningful phase and amplitude differences; preserve
connections and let residual motion resolve. Check the tip as well as the root.

**Exception:** a rigidly locked assembly can move together. Do not add arbitrary
delays to a planted hand or foot. Higher mass alone does not prescribe a longer
lag: the entire mechanical relationship matters.

## 6. Slow in and slow out

**Decide:** how the subject gains or loses speed. Use spacing to show an applied
force, controlled stop, gentle settle, or deliberately sharp stop.

**Recognize and repair:** equal spacing makes an organic action feel abrupt,
or identical easing makes every beat mushy. Shape the spacing for this action;
preserve contrast between preparation, acceleration, impact, and recovery.

**Exception:** free fall accelerates toward contact; collisions change velocity
rapidly. Constant velocity can be intentional. Prefer explicit words such as
"accelerate away" and "decelerate toward" because animation and UI terminology
can use slow-in/ease-in differently. See [timing](timing-and-motion.md).

## 7. Arcs

**Decide:** what path follows anatomy, a pivot, momentum, or graphic intent.
Track a visible landmark such as a wrist, nose, heel, or prop corner.

**Recognize and repair:** a limb cuts a corner, changes length, or makes a bump
between good keys. Add a purposeful breakdown or fix the pivot/space. Review
the projected path through the delivery camera.

**Exception:** mechanical constraints and designed graphic paths may be straight.
Do not bend a trajectory merely to satisfy an "organic" preference. Path shape
and the rate of travel along it are different decisions.

## 8. Secondary action

**Decide:** which additional behavior supports the main intention. A hesitant
look before knocking can change how the same knock is understood.

**Recognize and repair:** the action has no personality, or an embellishment
steals its meaning. Add a specific supporting choice, then give it suitable
timing and visibility. Remove it if it confuses the shot.

**Exception:** supporting action is optional. Hair swinging from the knock is
overlap, not by itself a secondary acting choice. Silence and stillness can
carry the personality.

## 9. Timing

**Decide:** when the action starts, changes, contacts, peaks, and resolves. Tune
the beat lengths to effort, thought, distance, rhythm, and emotional intent.

**Recognize and repair:** good poses communicate the wrong attitude or weight.
Try a materially different beat duration before polishing details. Contrast
quick actions with held information; do not space all beats equally.

**Exception:** more drawings only increase duration when exposure and playback
rate make them take more time. Adding intermediate samples within a fixed
duration does not slow the event. Heavy does not mean universally slow.

## 10. Exaggeration

**Decide:** which idea needs emphasis: direction, effort, emotion, contrast,
shape, or a brief impact. Push that idea while preserving its context.

**Recognize and repair:** correct movement reads weakly at speed. Strengthen a
key silhouette, sharpen timing contrast, or accent the relevant frame; compare
against a restrained version in motion.

**Exception:** not every channel should become larger. A quiet character moment
may benefit from less motion around a precise eye movement. Extreme stills can
work in a fast action but need playback review.

## 11. Solid drawing / solid posing

**Decide:** how volume, orientation, balance, and weight remain understandable.
Construct forms consistently in 2D; in 3D, a valid model still needs convincing
poses and clear silhouettes.

**Recognize and repair:** features swim, volumes collapse unintentionally, limbs
merge, or the body cannot support its pose. Recheck construction, contacts,
line of action, perspective, and how weight is carried.

**Exception:** flattening, foreshortening cheats, asymmetry, and stylized changes
may be intentional for a shot. Twinning is a diagnostic clue, not a prohibition
against symmetrical actions or poses. See [2D](2d.md) and [3D](3d.md).

## 12. Appeal

**Decide:** what makes the subject and performance distinctive and worth
following. Favor coherent shapes, a readable attitude, and purposeful contrast.

**Recognize and repair:** details obscure the character or every pose is generic.
Simplify distracting shapes, strengthen the defining posture, and make an
acting choice specific to this character.

**Exception:** appeal does not require beauty, cuteness, heroism, or friendliness.
An unsettling or awkward character can be compelling. Judge against the brief
and audience rather than a universal attractive-body template.
