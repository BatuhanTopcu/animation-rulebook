# Reference analysis and shot planning

Use evidence to resolve the decisions that matter. A motion reference, storyboard,
audio track, technical measurement, and visual style reference answer different
questions; one attractive still cannot establish all of them.

## Observe, explain, then adapt

Watch a reference at normal speed for intention and rhythm, then inspect the
specific interval that explains the movement. Identify support changes, visible
landmarks, gaze targets, and the action's outcome. Record timing in seconds unless
the source's playback rate and any retiming are known. Do not infer acceleration
from frame spacing in an unknown-speed clip or confuse camera movement with
subject movement.

Keep an observation separate from its explanation: "the heel releases before
the trunk rises" is visible evidence; the exact applied force is an inference.
A single view can hide depth, contacts, and occluded limbs. Use another view or
anatomical reference when the uncertainty affects the animation. Preserve the
causal relationship when adapting proportions, scale, or style rather than
tracing every coordinate.

For acting, reference should explore a character pursuing something, not an
inventory of hand poses. Ed Hooks' [masterclass outline](https://edhooks.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/AFA_Masterclass-Studios-game-companies-v4.pdf)
connects performance reference with intention and objectives. Record or select
alternative interpretations where useful; choose the one that communicates the
scene's thought. Do not require new reference capture for a trivial correction.

## Make a rough sequence answer real questions

Disney's [layout process](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/layout/) treats
rough character blocking, composition, cameras, and how shots cut together as
early storytelling decisions. Apply that principle in any tool: use thumbnails,
simple shapes, or inexpensive poses to test the event and its framing before
refining surfaces and small motion.

For a substantial sequence, a compact beat map can contain:

| Beat | Viewer needs to understand | Evidence/constraint | Motion consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | What changes the situation | Audio, contact, gaze, or event | Place the cue where it can be noticed |
| Response | What the subject chooses or undergoes | Intention or physical constraint | Set a specific pose/path and timing |
| Outcome | What is now different | Final state and next shot | Leave enough readable resolution |

This is a working aid, not a required schema. Preserve the user's chosen shot
length, narrative, and style; do not add a story arc to a simple loop or diagram.

## Use controlled comparisons

When a shot is uncertain, compare a small number of materially different choices:
for example an early versus delayed response, or two contact schedules. Keep
other major variables stable so the result answers the question. Choose based
on readability and intent, then refine. Do not generate decorative variations
that differ only in surface detail when the timing is the real uncertainty.

Review gates should prevent wasted work: the idea reads in blocking; mechanics
and performance work in preview; style and secondary layers support them; final
output preserves that result. Return to the earliest failing decision instead
of adding polish over it. Use [review](review.md) for evidence and completion.
