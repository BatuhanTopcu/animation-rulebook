# Acting, facial performance, and dialogue

## Give movement a reason

Specify the character's objective, obstacle, and current approach in ordinary
language: "get the cup back without admitting it matters" is more playable than
"look sad." An action can be withholding, inviting, testing, or avoiding. Let
an event or a failed approach motivate a change. In a conversation, the listener
also interprets what happens; silence is not a pause in their existence. These
are selective acting tools, not a claim that every reflex or abstract object
needs a psychological story. [Ed Hooks' acting outline](https://edhooks.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/AFA_Masterclass-Studios-game-companies-v4.pdf)
is the performance reference for objectives, obstacles, and active listening.

## Phrase the performance

Choose major pose changes around shifts of intention. Smaller changes inside
a sustained pose can carry uncertainty, attention, and emphasis. Contrast motion
with readable stillness rather than illustrating every word. Give a thought
enough time to register when the scene needs it; there is no mandatory pause
before every emotion. The spoken words and the unspoken intention may differ.
These decisions draw on Dana Boadway-Masson's [advanced performance guidance](https://www.animationmentor.com/blog/10-advanced-acting-performance-tips-for-animators/).

Design a characteristic resting attitude instead of using a rig's default
stance as personality. Keep intensity appropriate to shot size: a close-up can
carry a tiny gaze change that a wide shot cannot. Avoid treating a stock gesture,
fixed blink rate, or facial label as proof of a particular emotion.

## Coordinate gaze, face, and speech

Give the eyes a target and let changes of attention have context. Check gaze
convergence and eyelid contact in the actual view; small geometric errors can
overwhelm a quiet performance. Do not add random eye darts to imply thought.
Head/body leadership and blink placement are choices to review, not an automatic
eyes-first formula. Keep a supporting gesture from hiding the expressive region.

For speech, work from the actual recording and phrasing. A sound does not have
one immutable mouth shape: nearby sounds, articulation, and delivery affect the
visible result. Coordinate jaw and lips, preserve important closures, and allow
shapes to influence neighboring shapes instead of resetting to neutral between
phonemes. This follows the speech-animation concepts described by the authors
of [JALI](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~karan/jali/); it does not require their rig
or software. A mouth library is a starting vocabulary, not finished acting.

Judge lip timing in synchronized playback. Do not apply an assumed universal
two-frame lead to every voice or fps. Keep the expressive upper face related
to the performance rather than driving it solely from audio amplitude.

## A useful trial

For "I'm fine" after a disappointment, try a small protective posture, a brief
check of the other person's reaction, and a restrained answer. Compare it with
a version that changes pose on both words. Choose what reveals the intended
subtext; neither version is automatically correct. This is an original exercise,
not a timing prescription from a source.

Review with sound for phrasing and sync, then without sound for visible intent.
The silent pass cannot prove lip synchronization. If no audio or playback was
available, identify that limit. See [staging](staging-and-cameras.md) for who
owns the audience's attention through a dialogue exchange.
